Project Overview

The organization is an Egyptian retail confectionery chain operating 8 branches with 50–70 employees. Prior to this project, performance evaluation existed in name only — managers assessed staff informally, without defined KPIs, without consistent criteria across branches, and without any data trail that could support objective decisions on compensation, promotion, or development.

I identified this gap during my part-time HR role at the company, proposed a structured solution, secured management buy-in, and designed and deployed the entire system independently. The requirement was clear: the system had to be operational immediately, without any new software, training overhead, or dedicated HR staffing. It had to run on the tools already available — paper, Google Forms, and Excel.

What was delivered is a complete, multi-source performance evaluation framework covering four job roles across the organizational hierarchy. Every component — evaluation forms, digital input channels, a scoring engine, and monthly notification templates — is fully built and currently in active use across all branches.

02

Problem Statement

Performance evaluation was entirely informal and inconsistent. There were no role-defined KPIs, no standardized scoring process, and no structured data collection — which meant a cashier in one branch could be evaluated on entirely different criteria than a cashier in another, entirely at the discretion of whichever manager happened to be on shift that day.

This produced three documented organizational problems: compensation decisions with no objective basis, an inability to identify performance risks or development needs before they escalated, and no historical record of employee performance over time. Decisions were made on gut feeling, and there was no mechanism to challenge or audit them.

Compounding the issue, the organization had no HR infrastructure — no software, no dedicated HR staff, and no existing templates. Any solution had to be deployable on day one using existing resources.

03

Design Objective

Design and implement a complete Performance Management System that converts subjective impressions into structured, role-specific, weighted scores — producing a defensible, consistent output of 100 points per employee per month, regardless of which manager conducts the evaluation or which branch is being assessed.

The system must reduce single-evaluator bias through multi-source data collection, generate actionable output directly usable for compensation and development decisions, and be fully operational without any additional technology investment — while being architecturally structured to migrate to a digital platform without redesigning its core logic.

04

System Architecture

The system is structured across three independent, modular layers — collection, scoring, and reporting. The key architectural decision: each layer can be upgraded or replaced without touching the others. Paper forms become digital forms. The Excel engine becomes a database. The reports become a dashboard. The evaluation logic remains constant throughout.

Paper Evaluation Forms
4 role-specific forms — Branches Manager, Branch Manager, Salesperson, Cashier — each with weighted KPI categories, named data sources, and a 1–5 scale that resolves to a score out of 100.
Layer 1 — Collection
Google Forms — Digital Input
8 live forms: 4 evaluation submission forms and 4 end-of-month notification templates. Eliminates manual transcription errors and feeds responses directly into the scoring engine.
Layer 1 — Collection
Excel Scoring Engine
Aggregates multi-source inputs, applies role-specific weights, calculates a final score out of 100, and generates monthly performance summaries per employee and per branch — operational with zero additional software.
Layer 2 — Scoring
Monthly Performance Reports
Structured output per employee: total score, category breakdown, trend vs. prior month, and a narrative field for manager annotations — formatted for direct use in compensation and development decisions.
Layer 3 — Reporting
Multi-Source Evaluation
Data drawn from at least three independent sources per employee: direct manager observation, peer review, customer feedback, and operational system data (POS, Attendance, Cash Reports). No single evaluator controls the outcome.
Core Design Principle
Monthly Evaluation Cycle
Notification dispatched end-of-month → forms completed within 3 days → engine populated → scores calculated → reports issued to management → decisions made. Full cycle designed to close within one week.

Multi-Source Evaluation Design

The foundational design principle: no single evaluator controls the final score. Each employee's result is built from independent data streams, weighted by role relevance. This shifts performance conversations from subjective disagreement to structured dialogue around data — and makes the system auditable and defensible at every level of the hierarchy.

The 4 Evaluation Forms

Each form is built around the actual accountability scope of that role — what it produces, what data is available to measure it, and what genuinely matters for organizational performance at that level. A cashier's financial accuracy carries 40% because cash discrepancies are traceable and directly costly. A branch manager's team development rate carries weight because it predicts branch performance months in advance. The weights are not arbitrary — they reflect operational priority.

09

Lessons Learned

Data source design is the actual work
Naming a KPI takes minutes. Determining exactly where the data comes from, who is accountable for recording it, and at what frequency — that's what determines whether the system works in practice. Every indicator in this system has a named, available data source, or it doesn't exist in the design.
Weights make priorities explicit and testable
Assigning percentage weights converts implicit organizational priorities into a verifiable statement. Financial accuracy is 40% of a cashier's score because cash discrepancies are traceable and costly. Teamwork is 30% of a salesperson's score because the floor environment is inherently collaborative. The numbers don't allow vagueness.
Designing on paper forces clarity before technology
Every process decision in this system was made at the paper stage — before anything digital was touched. The Excel engine was straightforward to build because the evaluation logic was already fully defined. Technology should implement a clear process, not substitute for one that hasn't been thought through.
Multi-source makes the system defensible
When a score is produced by four independent data streams, performance conversations shift from subjective disagreement to structured dialogue around data. A manager's personal view becomes one input among several — not the final word. That shift is what gives the system organizational legitimacy.
